    - a blast from the past: the ***frame problem***
        - this is actually very simple: how to decide what variables even matter?
        - equivalent to choosing a model to do BI in
        - tons of other names for it: model search, structure learning, some types meta-learning are this, concept learning, in some sense causal inference, representation learning, etc.
        - but it’s very hard: you are trying to cut through a massive search space
            - for the computational complexity nerds out there: even worse, as a problem class the frame problem is almost certainly incomputable, as any solution to it would formalise the process by which formalisation happens in agents, strongly hinting at Gödelian issues
            - but we don’t lose much sleep over BI being NP-complete either. it just means there won’t be fully general solutions, but biological agents aren’t fully general either, so that’s fine
    - lotsa things are trying to solve something similar to this:
        - hierarchical Bayes, including program inference, neurath’s ship, and others
    - The reason these all end up being heuristics is that they try to solve an issue that is outside of BI from within
        - what does it mean to be outside? to use different information. RL is outside of BI because of the reward signal
        - but RL is difficult to use for the stated purpose for different reasons (and not for the lack of trying)
    - BI has this kinda flat view of the world, as one big statistician’s assignment
        - while there is a detailed agent-environment dynamic with boundaries and various constraints at play
        - a lot of the confusion in the bayesian literature comes from ignoring this structure I think
        - on top of the spatial structure of the boundaries, there is a lot of temporal structure too - the agents are doing tasks
- There are **attempts to say something about this structure** and dynamic of course
    - resource rationality does get at some of the additional structure
        - but it ends up focusing almost entirely on the agent’s internal architecture in terms of computational constraints
        - which is fair, but I think it does end up being the less interesting side of the coin
        - and while the other side, the task structure, is absolutely there in the mission statement, it ends up being neglected
    - RL is very rigid in its unrelenting focus on reward, and empirically does not lend itself easily to include a normative theory of the representational component
        - everything is either myopic gradient descent with no integral theory in sight, or explosively expensive planning which is vacuously normative
        - striking the middle ground is a rare feat, but there might still be interesting ground to cover here - this is what I did in Tübingen
    - one very agent-centric view is autopoiesis, that is based on the notion of finitude, i.e. the agent is what is combatting its own decomposition
        - different from autocathalitic and other self-reinforcing processes, because those don’t combat decomposition, as they burn through their fuel as fast as possible and then die
        - the algorithmic side is severely underdeveloped as you might have guessed
        - helpfully, autopoets use “representation” to mean the opposite of what I mean by it
    - somehow they all end up ***saying zilch*** about the most interesting part
- Characterising the **information flow** on the agent-environment boundary
    - this is the mutual information objective I talked about a ton already
    - and here comes the extra piece of information we consider: the ***time allotted*** for the learning task, or in other words, the number of observations that will be available
        - this is what gives rise to the discretisation phenomenon, that you can interpret as the emergence of proto-symbols, i.e. things to be considered as separate

        - this is similar to AP in this choice, different in practice from RR, and somewhat related to RL via the discount factor, albeit it is treated differently here
    - there are other IT ways to characterise the boundary, some more sophisticated, but we are largely ok with the simple one for now as we are talking about a passively learning agent
        - so e.g. affordances don’t come into the picture, which definitely puts us closer to humans than amoebae in terms of epistemic capacity
        - in the experiment this is a bit more complicated - we assume that there is an active component, that confers all the urgency of the situation, and a passive statistical learning component, that inherits it
    - the objective can be optimised in other ways, and is, albeit usually not the finite-data one
        - in terms of the likelihood function: efficient coding
        - in terms of the data: active learning

    - this space hints at bootstrapping processes
        - roweis and ghahramani best paper ever anyways
        - circling gödelian horrors prolly has to do this
- So how to construct a **process model** out of this?
    - The first main component is to be able to evaluate the objective at a non-initial point, when some learning already took place
        - two sources of information, one target, Venn-diagram
        - deriving the objective
        - the sampling trick, avoiding priors on the latent dynamic
        - intuiting some errors in the derivation
        - simulation results: the ***future gives the shape, the past the weighting***
            - is this actually intuitive?
            - main next question: *how to do this in such a way that doesn’t have this property*
    - The second main point is to stitch such steps together in a daisy chain

\section{Anatomy of a cost}

\begin{itemize}
\item First, there is some physical limitation. This could be time, space, storage capacity of various sorts, a bandwith limitation on some channel, codeword length ceiling, etc. (maybe it's good to have a comprehensive list of these?)
\item This will either directly manifest as a cost (if the constraint is soft), or can be turned into a cost using a Lagrange dual formalism on a hard constraint. This cost is unfortunately very often not known, and has to be included as a hyperparameter.
\item The cost can be in any units - can be bits, but also seconds, square meters, whatever. When it's balanced against a reward, the hyperparameter absorbs the unit conversion as well.
\item Taking the cost into account has informational \emph{consequences}, which are always in bits or related units. These consequences ares due to some form of memoisation going on in the agent - trying to learn a model, a policy, a value function, or just storing episodic memories are all introducing an intermediary variable into the reaction loop, that is continuously adapted, and thus acts as an information bottleneck between sensory input and the optimal choice of action. How much of the information in the sensory input can make it into the bottleneck variable is modulated by the cost - either directly, by limiting how much of it can be observed in the first place or how precisely it can be conveyed by the perception apparatus, or indirectly, via limiting e.g. how precisely a value function can be calculated based on the observations.
\item Thus, while the cost can be traded off directly against reward as well, one can always instead account for it through these informational consequences, using some kind of mutual information maximisation objective. And when the task is density estimation in the first place, that is expressible by maximising an inverse KL reward, which is a mutual information to begin with.
\item It is probably a good idea to always start modelling by enumerating the physical limitations, and either explicitly incorporate them via the information theoretic route, or argue why they won't have much influence in the particular setup of the study. Unfortunately this includes arguing that people don't have an ingrained algorithmic motif accounting for the cost to such degree that it is active regardless of task properties.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Possible constraints}

\begin{itemize}
\item Number of observations
\item Sensory precision
\item Number of computational steps to compute perceptual posterior (to really assess this one would also need to know what the available computational primitives are)
\item Availability of episodic or working memory slots
\item Precision of episodic or working memory slots upon encoding and/or recall
\item Storage-type limitations on the complexity of the learned model (or value function, policy, etc). This is also hard to assess, as one would need to know the parametric bases in which the agent can encode these.
\item Computation-type limitations on the update of a model
\item Computation-type limitations on the use of a model to compute a choice
\item Computational constraints on off-trial and off-task updates
\item Action noise
\item The bandwith of any internal channel between components
\item Homeostatic constraints on reward not baked into the reward function (e.g. don't die)
\end{itemize}
